## Article: Breakdown Of Ramp's $60B Outbound GTM Strategy

Ramp was valued at $60B in October 2026.

It does $2B+ in annualised revenue, for 85,000+ businesses.

And the part every outbound team should read twice:

In 2021, Ramp had 5 SDRs and millions of businesses to reach.

So they built their own AI SDR, years before you could buy one.

It wrote the emails, sorted the replies, and produced around 30% of Ramp's pipeline for years.

Then on December 2, 2025, they shut it down.

Today Ramp runs 150+ SDRs, and every one of them opens the same app every morning.

Our team has sent 17M+ cold emails for 100+ B2B companies, so I went through every public talk, post and write-up from Ramp's founders and sales leaders on how they sell.

This is every phase, in order, with the part you can steal at any size.

### The scoreboard first

- $60B valuation in October 2026, in a $1.85B round led by Dragoneer
- $2B+ in annualised revenue
- 85,000+ customers
- Around 500 people in sales
- Revenue forecast to within 5%
Their growth lead calls the first stretch the fastest climb in SaaS and fintech history: $100M in run rate in about 2 years.

None of that came from one clever trick.

It came from 7 phases, and each one fixed the problem the last one created.


### Phase 1: Sell to the people you already know

What they did:

Before launch, the founders interviewed around 100 finance people about how they managed company spend.

Then they put founders and operators who would use the product on the cap table.

Eight Sleep and Pop became early customers because their founders were investors.

The first deals came from warm intros, first through the founders' networks, then through the whole company's and the investors'.

They downloaded LinkedIn connections and matched them against the prospects they wanted, looking for anyone who knew someone.

Why it worked:

A warm intro skips the trust problem completely.

And early customers who already know you give honest feedback fast, which is what an early product needs most.

What to steal:

- Export your team's and investors' LinkedIn connections and match them against your ICP list
- Ask for the intro to one named person, with a 2-line blurb they can forward as is
- Treat every early customer as a source of the next 3 intros
Warm intros run out though.

Ramp found that out fast, and the next channel they found is the one everything else got built on.


### Phase 2: Find the channel that teaches you fastest

What they did:

Cold email became Ramp's first channel that worked again and again.

Their first SDR tested a lot of copy variations, looking for whatever got replies and meetings.

Megan Yen, one of Ramp's first 30 hires, gave 2 reasons they doubled down on it:

- It gave the fastest feedback on messaging of any channel, faster than SEO or paid ads
- Once a reply turned into a meeting, a lot of prospects became customers within 30 days
The offer underneath it:

Ramp's whole pitch is a company card that helps you spend less.

Their own numbers say the median customer cuts costs by 5%.

They even built a savings calculator that takes your company size and card spend and shows you the money.

Why it worked:

"Save money" is the easiest offer in B2B to believe, because the prospect can check it against their own numbers.

And cold email let them test which version of that offer landed in days instead of quarters.

What to steal:

The offers that win on cold email rank like this:

1. Make money
2. Save money
3. Save time
4. Lower risk
Ramp sells the second one, and puts a number on it.

Then use cold email as your research tool before you scale it:

- Keep every email identical apart from the offer line, so you know which offer did the booking
- Give each version 1,000 sends before you judge it
- Kill the bottom 60-70% after two weeks
Max Freeman, Ramp's first sales hire, said it plainly: "response rate isn't the right metric to index on."

We judge every campaign on contacts per lead instead (how many people you contacted for every interested reply).

Under 500 is a good spot.

At 2,000 or more, the offer is the problem, and no amount of copy changes fixes it.


### Phase 3: Hire people who are built to work

What they did:

Max Freeman joined in 2020 as Ramp's first sales hire.

He quit a mid-market AE job to become an SDR again, because he believed in the market Ramp was going after.

Then Ramp did something most startups wouldn't.

They hired investment bankers as early sellers and paid them around 2x a normal SDR.

In return, they expected 40 to 60 meetings a month, roughly 4 to 6x the norm.

His reasoning: "bankers are programmed to work."

A few other rules from the early team:

- Early reps had no quotas while the motion was still being figured out
- They never hired a single seller on their own
- Every candidate got the same test: "Would I buy a product from this person?"
Those early hires now run large parts of the business, and the team forecasts its revenue to within 5%.

Why it worked:

Pay above market, expect above market.

A rep who books 40+ meetings a month pays for the premium many times over.

What to steal:

For a smaller team, the seat that matters most is the one working the replies.

When a positive reply gets a call and they pick up, 80-90% book.

So hire for that seat first, pay for output, and hire 2 at a time so they can learn from each other's numbers.


### Phase 4: Build the AI SDR before anyone sells one

Here's how co-founder Gene Lee described the problem:

"We had a team of five SDRs that needed to reach out to millions of people."

So in 2021, Ramp built OATs, short for Outbound Automation Team.

What they built:

- A Snowflake database of Ramp's whole market, pulled together from dozens of sources (product data, Salesforce, HubSpot, Zendesk, Outreach and their ad platforms)
- An early version of ChatGPT drafting the emails and sorting the replies
- Tier 1 accounts went to human reps
- Tier 2 and Tier 3 accounts went through OATs, with no rep in the loop
- A growth engineering team owned the channel, and the engineers shared the sales quota
Their growth lead called it "unique to Ramp where the engineers feel ownership of the quota."

The result:

A 2022 dbt Labs case study put OATs at 25% of Ramp's pipeline.

Gene Lee later said it was roughly 30% "for years."

Why it worked:

The clever part was the market table.

Ramp had every account in its market in one place, scored and split into tiers, before a single automated email went out.

So the automation only ever wrote to accounts that were worth writing to.

What to steal:

Own your market before you automate any of it.

A raw database pull lands around 50-60% ICP fit.

We have AI read every company's website, write one line on what they sell, and check it against what the ICP is and isn't.

Anything under an 80% match gets cut, and the list ends up 90%+ accurate.

Then split it into tiers, so the expensive effort only goes where the deal size pays for it.

Here's the prompt we'd start with:

```
Here's my company list: [file].
Columns: company, domain, employees, industry, description.

My ICP: [who you sell to, deal size, must-haves, deal-breakers].

1. For every company, write one line on what they sell.
2. Score each one 0-100 against my ICP, with one line on why.
3. Cut anything under 80.
4. Split the rest into 3 tiers:
   Tier 1: the biggest likely deal size or a live buying signal
   Tier 2: a strong fit with no signal yet
   Tier 3: a fit, but small
5. Give me the count in each tier.

Don't invent any data. If you can't tell, mark it UNKNOWN.
```
Tier 1 gets a person.

Tiers 2 and 3 get the automated motion.


### Phase 5: Shut it down

On December 2, 2025, Gene Lee announced Ramp was shutting OATs down.

His reasons, in his own words:

- "dozens of automated outbound solutions, all operating on similar data which our competitors also have access to"
- "cold-outbound fatigue" on the buyer's side
- The sale had got more complex, with around 500 people in sales and several flagship products
At Clay's June 2026 Future of GTM livestream, Ramp's growth product lead Keyan Sarrafzadeh added the human side of it:

People buying complex financial products want some human connection.

Returns from the automated funnel had flattened, so Ramp merged it into the main sales funnel and spread its growth engineers across the GTM team.

What this tells you:

Ramp still uses AI all over its sales team.

What went was the AI sending emails to buyers with nobody checking them.

It matches what we hear on sales calls.

Around 80% of the sales teams we speak to cancelled their AI SDR inside 3 months, because it never produced pipeline.

But full automation still works in the right place.

For a custom cup manufacturer we work with, an AI agent answers every reply on its own, inside 30 seconds, at around 95% accuracy.

Their whole sales process runs over email, because local business owners don't want Zoom calls.

That campaign got 1,100 positive replies in 21 days.

So the rule we use is simple:

- Simple sale, replies that look alike: let the agent send
- Big deals, senior buyers, replies that need judgment: the agent drafts, a person presses send
Either way, the draft is ready in seconds.

### Phase 6: Give every rep a list with the reason on it

What replaced OATs is an internal app called Ramp Revenue.

What a rep sees every morning:

- A ranked list of the accounts to work today
- The reason each one is on the list
- A summary of the account and where the data came from
- A first email draft, which the rep reviews before anything sends
Keyan described it as "all the prospects that you should be focusing on, an explanation of why."

The signals behind the ranking:

- Pricing page visits
- New hires
- Webinar registrations
- In-person events
- Intent from other sites
- Competitor activity
Keyan estimates 99% of their SDRs log in every week, if not every day.

Then they went a step further.

Ramp built what it calls a Growth MCP, which lets each rep's own AI tools act on their behalf, with permissions and usage limits.

300 to 400 of their salespeople were building their own tools in Claude Code and Codex.

Every action those tools take gets logged with the reason the AI gave, and the growth team groups those reasons to find which workflows lead to the best results.

Why it worked:

The AI does the research and the first draft.

A person decides what the buyer reads.

And every account on the list has a reason to be contacted today.

What to steal:

Work your signals first, because a signal answers "why now?" before the prospect asks it.

For a New York financial services firm we work with, a provider in their market got acquired.

That left every company using the acquired provider needing a new one.

So we emailed all of them about the acquisition, with 2 years of free service as the offer.

106 interested leads from 23,418 people contacted.

You don't need a product team to build a basic version of Ramp's morning list.

This prompt in Claude Code gets you most of the way:

```
Here's my qualified account list: [file].
Here's this week's signal data: [file].
Signals: pricing page visits, new hires in [role], funding,
job posts mentioning [problem], competitor contract renewals.

1. Match every signal to an account on my list.
2. Rank the accounts by how many signals they have, then by tier.
3. For the top 25, write:
   - one line on why they're on the list today
   - the best contact to email (name, title)
   - a first email under 65 words that mentions the signal
     without sounding like we're watching them
4. Save it as a table I can review.

Send nothing. Keep everything as drafts.
```
Read every draft before it goes out.

That's the part Ramp put back in human hands.


### Phase 7: Cap how much of the market you touch

This is the phase that gets skimmed, and it's Keyan's top outbound recommendation.

What they do:

- Ramp caps the share of its total market it contacts each month
- Monthly volume goals are set to stop them burning out the market
- Quality gets tracked in a set order: positive reply rate first, then how many replies turn into booked demos
- Reps get targets and leaderboards on both
And outbound to the same audience performs better now than it did 2 years ago, because the brand has grown while they protected the market.

Why it worked:

A market you hit every week stops replying.

A market you hit on a schedule, with a new angle each time, keeps producing.

What to steal:

We contact every account in a client's market every 30 to 60 days, and never more often than that.

Before any segment goes first, we check its size, because a perfect segment with 1,000 companies in it gets used up in weeks.

If the whole market is under 10,000 companies, skip the volume play and work it account by account.

And before every pass, the list gets re-enriched, because 30-50% of the contacts on a 6-month-old list have moved jobs or changed their details.

Someone who said no 3 months ago can be ready today.


### What a $3M+ ARR company should copy

1. Own your market in one table before you automate anything.Every account, scored and tiered, before the first email.
2. Find your offer with cold email. Judge it on contacts per lead, not reply rate.
3. Put software on the research and the first draft. Keep a person on anything a buyer reads in a complex sale.
4. Work signals first. Every account on today's list needs a reason to be contacted today.
5. Cap how often you hit each account. Every 30 to 60 days, with a fresh angle and a re-enriched list.
6. Give someone a pipeline number they own. At Ramp it was the engineers, and at your size it can be one GTM engineer.

### Where teams go wrong copying Ramp

Buying an AI SDR to replace the team. Ramp built theirs on top of a full market database, ran it for years, and still shut it down once the sale got complex.

Hiring 150 SDRs before the offer converts. Ramp found its offer with a handful of reps first, then scaled the team.

Collecting signals with no list underneath them. A pricing page visit means nothing if the company was never a fit.

Ramp spent 5 years and a full product team building this.

You can run the same order with one table, a few signals and one person on the replies.
