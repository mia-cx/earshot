# Literature: Writing that gets read

Research notes behind `content/writing/`. Every source below was opened and read for this pass, unless it sits under "Not verified". Claim checks mark each number or factual claim in the lessons as **confirmed**, **disputed** or **unverified**.

## Sources read

| Source | What it says (paraphrased) |
| --- | --- |
| [Stanford Magazine: Getting Attention (2007)](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/getting-attention) | Profile of Chip Heath and *Made to Stick*. The curse of knowledge: the more you know, the less you can picture not knowing it. Elizabeth Newton's tapper study: tappers predicted listeners would name 50% of songs; listeners named 3 of 120 (2.5%). Sticky ideas share simplicity, unexpectedness, concreteness, credibility, emotion and story. |
| [Hoa Loranger, NN/g: Plain Language Is for Everyone, Even Experts (2017)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/) | Usability study with experts in science, tech and medicine. They also wanted short, scannable text and disliked dense, jargon-heavy pages. Suggested reading level: 6th to 8th grade for general audiences, 10th to 12th for specialists. Sentences of 15 to 20 words at most. "No one has ever complained that a text was too easy to understand." |
| [Jakob Nielsen, NN/g: How Users Read on the Web (1997)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/) | 79% of test users scanned new pages; 16% read word by word. Concise text tested 58% more usable, scannable 47%, objective (no hype) 27%, all three together 124%. Users disliked promotional language. |
| [Kara Pernice, NN/g: F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web (2017)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/) | People scan in an F shape when text has no strong cues, they want to be efficient, and they aren't committed to reading every word. Seen on desktop and mobile. Counter it: most important points in the first two paragraphs, headings and lines that lead with the informative words, bullets, bold key phrases, cut what isn't needed. |
| [Kate Moran, NN/g: Reading Content on Mobile Devices (2016)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/) | 276 participants. Comprehension on phones was no worse than on computers in practice, but hard passages took longer to read on a phone. "For the majority of mobile content scenarios, the need for brevity and prioritization is still critical." |
| [Therese Fessenden, NN/g: Scrolling and Attention (2018)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/) | Eyetracking on web pages: 57% of viewing time above the fold, 74% in the first two screenfuls, 81% in the first three. Attention drops with every screen down. |
| [George Orwell: Politics and the English Language (1946)](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/) | Six rules: no stale figures of speech; never a long word where a short one will do; if a word can be cut, cut it; prefer active to passive; plain English over jargon; break any rule sooner than write something barbarous. |
| [Twitter: SimClusters README in the-algorithm (2023)](https://github.com/twitter/the-algorithm/blob/main/src/scala/com/twitter/simclusters_v2/README.md) | About 145,000 communities found from the follow graph of the top 20M producers. A tweet's embedding starts empty and adds the interest vector of each user who likes it, so a post's community placement comes from who engages with it. |
| [Christensen Institute: Jobs to Be Done](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/) | A job is "the progress they're trying to make" in a particular circumstance. People pull products into their lives to make progress. |
| [George Loewenstein: The Psychology of Curiosity (Psychological Bulletin, 1994)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) | Curiosity is a form of deprivation caused by noticing a gap in one's knowledge. Read via the abstract only: the full-text scan available online has no text layer. |
| [Marianne Aubin Le Quéré and J. Nathan Matias: When curiosity gaps backfire (Scientific Reports, 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-81575-9) | Registered report. Meta-analysis of 8,977 headline A/B tests from the Upworthy Research Archive (2013 to 2015). When the baseline headline is too vague, a more concrete one gets more clicks; when it is already concrete, more concrete gets fewer. Just the right amount of information maximises clicks. Of nine earlier lab and field studies of curiosity-gap headlines, three found a negative effect, two positive, four none. Curiosity peaks at medium confidence (an inverted U). |
| [John Caples: Tested Advertising Methods (1932; 5th edition revised by Fred E. Hahn, 1997)](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3350190W) | Ogilvy's foreword: the most effective headlines appeal to self-interest or give news; specifics are more believable than generalities. Caples: "curiosity alone is seldom enough to make a good headline", but curiosity added to a self-interest headline helps; write a number of headlines for every ad and pick the best. Read in the 5th edition text. |
| [BJ Fogg: Fogg Behavior Model](https://behaviormodel.org/), [Prompts](https://behaviormodel.org/prompts/), [Ability](https://behaviormodel.org/ability/) | B = MAP: motivation, ability and a prompt must meet at the same moment. "Without a Prompt, the target behavior will not happen." Fogg lists "call to action" as another name for a prompt. Making the behaviour smaller and simpler raises ability. |
| [Influence at Work (Robert Cialdini): Principles of Persuasion](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/) | Consistency: people act in line with what they've already said or done, so ask for small initial commitments. Residents who first agreed to a small window card were four times as likely to accept a large Drive Safely sign (the Freedman and Fraser 1966 study). |
| [Jenni Romaniuk interview, Contagious (2022)](https://contagious.cz/images/IO/jenni-romaniuk-on-distinctive-assets.pdf) | Distinctiveness is "looking like you and not like others" so people know at once that something is from you, and attach it to you in memory. Keep the form consistent while you build an asset; change weakens it. Don't make assets "fashion items". Creatives drawing from the same environment drift into sameness. |
| [Kate Moran, NN/g: The Four Dimensions of Tone of Voice (2016)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions/) | Tone varies on four scales: funny to serious, formal to casual, respectful to irreverent, enthusiastic to matter-of-fact. |
| [Kate Moran, NN/g: The Impact of Tone of Voice on Users' Brand Perception (2016)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users/) | Survey of 100 US adults on paired pages that differed only in tone. Trustworthiness explained 52% of willingness to recommend. Casual, conversational, moderately enthusiastic tones did best. A playful tone lowered trust for insurance; "Humor is extremely risky." |
| [Maurice Jakesch, Jeffrey Hancock and Mor Naaman: Human heuristics for AI-generated language are flawed (PNAS, 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07271) | Six experiments, 4,600 participants: people could not tell AI-written self-presentations from human ones. They relied on wrong cues (first-person pronouns and family topics read as human), so AI text can seem "more human than human". Read via the arXiv abstract. |
| [Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser: Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content (Science Advances, 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290) | 300 writers, 600 evaluators. Stories written with AI ideas were rated more novel and useful, mostly for less creative writers, but were more similar to each other (10.7% more similar with one AI idea). Read via the abstract and the [ScienceDaily release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/07/240712222127.htm); the journal page blocked automated access. |

## Make the first line earn the second

Principles: the information gap (Loewenstein), the right-sized gap (Aubin Le Quéré and Matias), self-interest beats curiosity alone and testing many headlines (Caples).

| Claim | Verdict | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A hook works by opening a gap the reader wants closed | **Confirmed** | Loewenstein 1994; Aubin Le Quéré and Matias 2025 |
| Specific beats vague | **Confirmed with a limit**: more concrete wins only while the headline is vague; past a point more detail loses clicks | Aubin Le Quéré and Matias 2025; Ogilvy in Caples |
| The five levers are equal; curiosity-gap hooks work | **Disputed**: Caples ranks self-interest and news first and calls curiosity alone seldom enough. Lab and field studies of curiosity-gap headlines split 3 negative, 2 positive, 4 null. Added a Watch out. | Caples; Aubin Le Quéré and Matias 2025 |
| The strongest hooks stack two or three levers | **Confirmed** for curiosity plus self-interest | Caples |
| Write 5 to 10 versions, keep one | **Confirmed** in principle ("write a number of headlines ... select the best one"). The number 5 to 10 is the post's. | Caples |
| Author: 1B+ lifetime impressions; +9,000 followers and ~6M impressions in the first month | **Unverified**, self-reported. Another post by the same author gives 8,000+ followers and 4M+ impressions for the first four weeks; the lesson now says the figures vary. | @ryangrowthh posts |
| Video hook is the first 1 to 2 seconds | Unverified | @ryangrowthh |

## Shape posts for skimmers

Principles: people scan (Nielsen 1997), the F-pattern and front-loading (Pernice 2017), brevity on mobile (Moran 2016), cut words and prefer active voice (Orwell 1946), people can't spot AI text reliably (Jakesch et al. 2023).

| Claim | Verdict | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readers scan before they read | **Confirmed**: 79% scanned | Nielsen 1997 |
| Most people read on their phone | Unverified for any one audience | @ryangrowthh |
| Short paragraphs, one thought per line, lists help scanners | **Confirmed** (scannable +47%, combined +124%) | Nielsen 1997; Pernice 2017 |
| Show proof and the point early | **Confirmed**: most important points in the first two paragraphs | Pernice 2017 |
| The last line is the second most important line | Unverified, the author's opinion | @ryangrowthh |
| If deleting a word doesn't change the meaning, delete it | **Confirmed** as a classic rule | Orwell 1946 |
| Use active voice; simple words | **Confirmed** | Orwell 1946 |
| Strong verbs: "grew really quickly" → "exploded" | **Disputed**: readers distrust hype; objective wording tested 27% more usable. The example is now an exact verb. | Nielsen 1997 |
| Em dashes and "it's not X, it's Y" are AI tells | **Disputed** as a detection rule: people can't reliably tell AI text apart and use wrong cues. Kept as a style choice with that caveat. | Jakesch et al. 2023 |

## Ask for one thing at the end

Principles: a call to action is a prompt, and ease raises the odds (Fogg); small commitments lead to larger ones (Cialdini, Freedman and Fraser); attention is highest early on the page (Fessenden, NN/g).

| Claim | Verdict | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readers tend to do what a post tells them to do | **Confirmed** in principle: without a prompt the behaviour doesn't happen. The size of the effect for posts is untested. | Fogg |
| Keep the ask low effort | **Confirmed** | Fogg (ability) |
| Ask strangers for small things, warm readers for big ones | **Confirmed** in principle | Cialdini (consistency) |
| An early ask reaches more readers | **Confirmed** for web pages (57% of viewing time above the fold); not measured on social feeds | Fessenden 2018 |
| Post the call to action as a reply under your best post | Unverified | @ryangrowthh |

## Build a voice nobody can copy

Principles: distinctiveness and consistency of form (Romaniuk), tone and trust (Moran), AI text is hard to detect (Jakesch et al.), AI help makes work more alike (Doshi and Hauser).

| Claim | Verdict | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formats and hooks can be copied, your voice can't | **Partly confirmed**: being recognisably yourself is what lets people attach each post to you, and shared cues are weaker. Voice can be imitated, so the lesson says "harder to copy". | Romaniuk 2022 |
| Stay consistent with how you come across | **Confirmed** while building: changing the form weakens it | Romaniuk 2022 |
| Choose smart, funny or blunt | Partly: tone has four dimensions; humour can cost trust | Moran 2016 (both) |
| Polish no longer tells you anything about quality | **Confirmed** in spirit: people can't tell AI text from human text | Jakesch et al. 2023; @addyosmani |
| Use AI as an editor, not an author | Unverified as a tested method; consistent with AI ideas making stories more alike | Doshi and Hauser 2024 |
| Writer bot: close to half of time rewriting AI output, target 20% | Unverified, one person's report with no result yet | @huntkyle |
