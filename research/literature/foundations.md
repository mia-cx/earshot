# Foundations: literature notes

Module `content/foundations/`. Every source below was opened and read on 2026-11-23. Paraphrases, not quotes, unless marked. A claim marked **unverified** has no source here and stays out of the lessons, or stays in labelled as one person's claim.

## 1. Attention is the job

### Principles and sources

- **Brands grow mainly by winning more buyers, which takes mental and physical availability.** Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, [How do you measure How Brands Grow?](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/how-do-you-measure-how-brands-grow). Summarises Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (2010): brands compete with every brand in the category and grow mainly through penetration (more buyers). Mental availability: buyers easily think of the brand when buying and recognise it quickly. Physical availability: buyers can buy it in a variant, quantity and price that fits.
- **Growth and decline come mostly from acquisition, not retention.** Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, [Answering critics](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/answering-critics) (Byron Sharp). Cites Riebe, Wright, Stern & Sharp, "How to grow a brand: Retain or acquire customers?", *Journal of Business Research* 67(5), 2014, 990–997 ([DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2013.08.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2013.08.005), metadata checked on Crossref; full text paywalled, not read): sales growth and decline were largely due to over- or under-performance in acquisition. Also says bigger brands have slightly higher loyalty on every metric (Double Jeopardy), so "brands grow only by acquisition" is a misreading.
- **Long-term brand building and short-term activation both pay; the best mix leaned to brand.** IPA, [The next chapter for The Long and the Short of It](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/ipa-blog/the-next-chapter-for-the-long-and-the-short-of-it) (Alison Hoad, 2023). Les Binet and Peter Field's 2013 IPA report found the best budget ratio was 60:40 brand building to activation; their 2018 *Effectiveness in Context* found 62:38. As short-term campaigns rose, effectiveness (very large business effects in IPA cases) fell.
- **Marketing starts at the product.** Seth Godin, [The circles of marketing](https://seths.blog/2012/07/the-circles-of-marketing/) (2012). Outer circle: hype and promotion. Next in: a story that resonates with a tribe. Next in: what surrounds the product (usability, support, atmosphere). Centre: the product itself. "When in doubt, when your marketing isn't working, the answer is easy: go one circle in." (quote)
- **Creator economy forecast.** Goldman Sachs Research, [The creator economy could approach half-a-trillion dollars by 2027](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/the-creator-economy-could-approach-half-a-trillion-dollars-by-2027) (19 April 2023, Eric Sheridan): total addressable market could roughly double to $480B by 2027 from $250B "today".
- **Creators vs traditional media in ad revenue.** The Guardian, [Social media creators to overtake traditional media in ad revenue this year](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2025/jun/10/social-media-creators-to-overtake-traditional-media-in-ad-revenue-this-year) (10 June 2025): reports WPP Media's forecast that content on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram will attract more ad income in 2025 than traditional media content.

## 2. Say what you are, for whom, against what

### Principles and sources

- **Positioning is context: how your product is a leader at delivering something a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about.** April Dunford, [A Quickstart Guide to Positioning](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning) (2021), summary of *Obviously Awesome* (2019). Five components: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, value, target customers, market category. Each depends on the others. Start with competitive alternatives ("what would customers do if our solution didn't exist?"), then what you have that they lack, then the value, then who cares most, then the category. Credits Christensen's jobs to be done for the starting point. Traps: listing "phantom competitors" nobody actually compares you with; forgetting "do nothing", which is the status quo you must position against (Dunford puts enterprise software's loss to "no decision" at about 25% of deals, an estimate).
- **The positioning statement is the wrong tool; positioning is a choice among several.** April Dunford, [Obviously Awesome: a product positioning exercise](https://hackernoon.com/obviously-awesome-a-product-positioning-exercise-604e8ced841e) (2016). Any product can be positioned several ways; a fill-in-the-blanks statement hides that. Earlier five-step version: let go of where you came from, isolate uniqueness, value, who cares, market frame of reference. Database example: repositioned from "database" to "business intelligence" to escape comparison with Oracle.
- **Positioning lives in the prospect's mind.** Al Ries, [The History of Positioning](https://ries.com/about/history-of-positioning). Ries and Jack Trout's series "The Positioning Era Cometh" ran in *Advertising Age* in April and May 1972; the idea was a word or concept prospects instantly accept, like "the leading toothpaste". The 1981 book *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* was not opened (**not verified** beyond the history page).
- **Few buyers see brands as different.** Jenni Romaniuk, Byron Sharp and Andrew Ehrenberg, ["Evidence concerning the importance of perceived brand differentiation"](https://byronsharp.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2008/04/different1.pdf), *Australasian Marketing Journal* 15(2), 2007. Across 17 markets in two countries, an average 11% of a brand's own customers called it "different", 10% "unique", 17% either. Exceptions had obvious functional differences (Aldi 67% "different"). 77% of Apple computer users did not see their brand as different or unique. Conclusion: perceived differentiation is not necessary to be bought; put distinctiveness (being easy to identify) at the centre of brand strategy.
- **Differentiation vs distinctiveness.** Byron Sharp, [Differentiation vs distinctiveness](https://byronsharp.wordpress.com/2008/04/10/differentiation-vs-distinctiveness/) (2008). Differentiation is a reason to buy; distinctiveness is the brand looking like itself. A new functional benefit is "plain old differentiation"; distinctiveness is branding and takes time.
- **Jobs to be done: people hire products to make progress in a circumstance.** Clayton Christensen Institute, [Jobs to Be Done theory](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/). A job is the progress people try to make toward a goal in particular circumstances, with functional, social and emotional sides. Circumstances explain choices better than demographics or product attributes. HBR's 2016 article by Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan, ["Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done"](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done), was paywalled; only its summary was read.

## 3. Research a market in an afternoon

### Principles and sources

- **AI-generated research is for hypotheses, not decisions.** Maria Rosala and Kate Moran, NN/g, [Synthetic Users: If, When, and How to Use AI-Generated "Research"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/synthetic-users/) (2024). Synthetic users give shallow or overly favourable feedback. Useful for desk research and generating hypotheses; real research needs real users.
- **Ask about specific past behaviour, not opinions about your idea.** Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (2013), [book site](https://www.momtestbook.com/). The three rules (checked against Michael Lynch's [book notes](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/), the book itself was not opened): talk about their life instead of your idea; ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future; talk less and listen more. Compliments are worthless data. Ask what the problem costs them now, not what they would pay.
- **What buyers say and what buyers did differ.** Christensen Institute, [Jobs to Be Done theory](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/), Bob Moesta's condominium case. Prospects and focus groups said they didn't need a formal dining room, and bay windows didn't lift sales. Interviews with actual buyers kept coming back to the dining room table: people moved once they knew what to do with it. Larger dining rooms, moving services and two years of storage followed, and prices went up $3,500.
- **Reviews hold the buyer's own words.** Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers, [Amazon review mining for copywriting](https://copyhackers.com/2014/10/amazon-review-mining/) (2014). Before mining, ask: who is the audience, what do they want, what are they using today, where are those solutions found. A headline lifted from a book review ("If you think you need rehab, you do") beat the control by more than 400% in clicks and more than 20% in form submits on the next page (Wiebe's own test, one client).
- **Ad libraries must show when each ad ran.** EU Digital Services Act, [Article 39](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_39.html) (Regulation 2022/2065; text read on an unofficial mirror, EUR-Lex blocked the fetch). Very large platforms must keep a public ad repository for as long as an ad runs and one year after, including the ad's content, who paid, and the period it ran. Spend and results are not on the required list. Meta's own Ad Library help pages render only with JavaScript and could not be read (**not verified**).

## 4. Fit your strategy on one page

### Principles and sources

- **Strategy is a diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent actions. Goals are not strategy.** Richard Rumelt, [The perils of bad strategy](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-perils-of-bad-strategy), *McKinsey Quarterly* (June 2011), adapted from *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* (2011). Four hallmarks of bad strategy: failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, bad strategic objectives, fluff. The kernel: a diagnosis that names the critical challenge, a guiding policy for overcoming it, and coordinated actions. Without a named obstacle you have "a stretch goal or a budget or a list of things you wish would happen". Read through the Wayback Machine because mckinsey.com blocked the fetch.
- **Focus on controllable inputs to move outputs.** Jeff Bezos, [2009 letter to shareholders](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312510082914/dex991.htm). Amazon spends little time debating financial outputs and focuses on "the controllable inputs to our business", which it believes is the best way to maximise outputs over time. Of 452 goals for 2010, "revenue" appears eight times, "free cash flow" four.
- **The GACC brief.** Emily Kramer, MKT1, [The GACC Marketing Brief](https://newsletter.mkt1.co/p/the-gacc-marketing-brief-the-best) (2021). Goals, audience, creative (unique take), channels (distribution); evolved while Kramer led marketing at Asana. Everything a team makes should ladder up to a goal or a perception and pass an "add value" test. The rest of the post is paywalled; the reason for the order was not read.
- **Bounce rate in GA4.** Google, [Engagement rate and bounce rate](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12195621?hl=en). Bounce rate is the share of sessions that were not engaged (under 10 seconds, no key event, one page or screen).
- SMART goals trace to George T. Doran, "There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives", *Management Review*, November 1981. The available PDF is a scan that could not be read (**not verified**; kept out of the lesson).

## 5. Map the whole funnel

### Principles and sources

- **Pirate metrics.** Dave McClure, [Startup Metrics for Pirates: AARRR!](https://www.slideshare.net/dmc500hats/startup-metrics-for-pirates-long-version) (2007). Acquisition (users arrive from channels), Activation (a happy first visit), Retention (they come back), Referral (they refer others), Revenue (they pay). The lesson's five stages split activation into onboarding and engagement and leave referral to the acquisition row.
- **A flat retention curve is the best sign of product-market fit.** Brian Balfour, [Building a Growth Framework Towards a $100 Million Product](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/hubspot-growth-framework-100m). Retention curves that flatten show users get real value over time; early on, most time goes into proving that fit.
- **Growth comes mostly from acquisition.** Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, [Answering critics](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/answering-critics), citing Riebe et al. 2014 (see lesson 1): sales growth and decline were largely due to acquisition, not retention. Loyalty rises a little as brands grow (Double Jeopardy), so the two move together.
