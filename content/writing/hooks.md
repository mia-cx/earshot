> [!TLDR]
> - A hook has one job: get the next line read. It opens a gap between what the reader knows and what they want to know.
> - The gap has to be the right size. Too vague or too complete, and they scroll on.
> - Lead with what the reader gets. Curiosity alone is the weakest pull.
> - Write the hook last, in 5 to 10 versions, then cut.

## A hook gets the next line read

The first line doesn't have to sell anything. Its only job is to **get the second line read**.

It works by opening a right-sized gap. Psychologist George Loewenstein [described curiosity](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) as the itch of noticing a gap in what you know. Marianne Aubin Le Quéré and J. Nathan Matias tested that in [a meta-analysis of 8,977 headline A/B tests](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-81575-9) from Upworthy. More specific headlines won more clicks when the original was vague, and fewer clicks when it already said enough. In Upworthy's archive, more detail would have cost clicks on about half the headlines and helped fewer than 1 in 10. Give enough detail to make the gap worth closing, and save the answer for line two.

- Lead with the most interesting thing. Cut the warm-up. If your best line is at the bottom, move it to the top.
- Be specific. "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month" beats "I grew fast". Numbers, names and timeframes beat vague promises, until the line gives the whole answer away.
- Answer "what's in it for me?" Keep it short. Create tension with a question, a surprise or a contradiction.

## Pull at least one lever

Every working hook pulls at least one of five levers: self-interest, curiosity, novelty, emotion (surprise, fear, FOMO, excitement) or identity ("people like me"). The strongest stack two or three.

The levers aren't equal. In the ad tests behind [*Tested Advertising Methods*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3350190W), John Caples found self-interest and news headlines won. Curiosity alone was "seldom enough", though it strengthened a self-interest headline.

Example: "If you're a founder under 1K followers, this one change doubled my reach." The first half is identity. The second is self-interest plus curiosity.

> [!TRICK] Pick from eight hook types
> 1. Bold claim: "Copywriting is the most underrated skill on 𝕏."
> 2. Callout: "If you're a founder with under 1K followers, read this."
> 3. Curiosity gap: "I just realized something 99% of founders miss."
> 4. Clear outcome: "How to write posts like the top 1%."
> 5. Contrarian: "F\*ck posting volume. You NEED to post less."
> 6. Story: "It took me 8 years to realize this..."
> 7. Number or list: "7 copywriting rules I wish I knew 6 years ago."
> 8. Proof: "This post got 2M views. I analyzed why (takeaways)."
>
> The author reports 1B+ lifetime impressions on X, and +9,000 followers and about 6M impressions in the first month of a new account. Another of their posts gives 8,000+ followers and 4M+ impressions for the same weeks. Those are their own figures, not a study.

> [!RISK] A curiosity gap on its own is a gamble
> Studies of curiosity-gap headlines disagree. Of the nine lab and field experiments Aubin Le Quéré and Matias list, three found curiosity-style headlines got fewer clicks than plain summaries, two found more, and four found no difference. Facebook also changed its feed to show fewer curiosity-gap headlines. Pair the gap with what the reader gets, as Caples advised.

## Put the hook where the format needs it

A hook isn't always the first sentence. In a video it's the first two seconds; in an article it's three separate pieces. Match it to the format.

> [!TRICK] Fit the hook to the format
> ```
> Short posts   → The hook IS the post. Make line one strong enough to stand alone.
> Threads       → Hook + promise of what's coming ("Here are 7 lessons:")
> Articles      → Title + subtitle + first paragraph all need to hook. Treat each as its own hook.
> Video         → The first 1 to 2 seconds. Visual movement + text on screen + spoken hook.
> Quote posts   → Your take needs to add tension or value to the original, not just "this."
> Images        → The headline on the image is the hook. One idea, readable at phone size.
> ```

## Write the hook last

You don't know what a post promises until it's written. Draft the body first, then write several hooks and cut down to one.

Caples gives the same advice in *Tested Advertising Methods*: "Write a number of headlines for every advertisement and then select the best one." Between 2013 and 2015, Upworthy ran over 27,000 A/B tests, many pitting rival headlines for the same story against each other.

> [!TRICK] Write 5 to 10 hooks, keep one
> 1. Write the post first, hook last.
> 2. Write 5 to 10 versions using different hook types.
> 3. Cut the weakest immediately.
> 4. Refine each survivor until every line rolls into the next.
> 5. Cut words that repeat or add nothing.

> [!FORM] Your hooks
> 1. First hook: name its type, then write the line.
> 2. Second hook, using a different type.
> 3. Third hook, using a different type.
> 4. Fourth hook, using a different type.
> 5. Fifth hook, using a different type.
> 6. Which one would you stop scrolling for?

> [!DO] Write five hooks for one post
> Take a post you've drafted or already published. Fill in your hooks above: five new first lines for it, each using a different type from the list of eight, and the one you'd stop scrolling for. Then download all five.

> [!CHECK] What makes a reader want the next line, and when does it fail?
> A gap between what they know and what they want to know. It fails when the gap is the wrong size: too vague to care about, or so complete there's nothing left to find out.

> [!CHECK] Your hook says "I grew fast." How do you make it stronger?
> Make it specific with a number, a name or a timeframe, as in "I hit 5.8M impressions in my first month." Specifics win when the hook is vague, but once it says enough, more detail costs clicks.

> [!CHECK] Why should you write a post's hook after the body?
> You don't know what the post promises until it's written. Then you write 5 to 10 hooks and cut to the best one.
