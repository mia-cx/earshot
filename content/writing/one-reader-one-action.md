> [!TLDR]
> - The more you know, the harder it is to write for someone who doesn't. Pick one reader, one action and one takeaway before you write.
> - One idea per post. Three ideas are three posts.
> - Keep the idea advanced and the words simple enough for a 12-year-old. Experts prefer plain words too.
> - Tell the change your product makes, from the user's point of view.

## Pick the action before you write

Once you know something, it's hard to imagine not knowing it. Chip and Dan Heath call this the curse of knowledge in *Made to Stick*, after [Elizabeth Newton's Stanford study](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/getting-attention): people tapping out well-known songs expected listeners to name half, and listeners named 3 out of 120. Naming one reader, one action and one takeaway first is how you catch yourself.

Copywriting is getting the right person to take the right action: read, reply, share, follow or buy. So **decide the action before you write the first word**. Everything else in the post serves that one action.

The "right person" is the reader you pictured in *Say what you are, for whom, against what*. The action gets its own lesson: *Ask for one thing at the end*.

## Keep one idea per post

A post with one idea is easier to remember and easier to pass on.

It also helps the feed. X's [published SimClusters code](https://github.com/twitter/the-algorithm/blob/main/src/scala/com/twitter/simclusters_v2/README.md) groups accounts into about 145,000 communities by who follows whom, and places each post by who likes it. A post about three topics draws likes from mixed communities, so it fits none of them well. That last step is one author's reading of the code, not a test. *How X picks what people see* covers the feed in detail.

Every post needs three things: a clear takeaway, a specific reader and a specific call to action. If you have three ideas, you have three posts.

## Choose clarity over cleverness

Good writing removes friction. Use vocabulary a 12-year-old can read, even when the idea is advanced. Keep the concept intact; simplify the words and the formatting.

Hoa Loranger at [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/) tested pages with experts in science, technology and medicine, and they also wanted short, plain, scannable text. NN/g suggests a 6th to 8th grade reading level (ages 11 to 14) for a general audience. "No one has ever complained that a text was too easy to understand."

Before you post, ask: "What does someone walk away with?" If you can't say it in a sentence, the post isn't ready.

## Write for the layperson and the expert

Picture a magician doing a trick nobody has ever done. A non-magician says "whoa, cool." The magician sitting next to them loses their mind, because they know it's never been done.

Aim for that. Write something a layperson finds interesting and an expert finds ten times more so. Copy written for a general audience still lands with experts, as Loranger's study shows, but copy written only for experts loses everyone else. Check each draft: would a non-specialist stop for this?

## Tell the change from the user's side

The [Christensen Institute's jobs-to-be-done research](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/) says people don't just buy products; they "pull them into their lives to make progress". So a story only works when the product makes a real change in someone's life.

1. Find the moment things changed for one user.
2. Describe their situation just before it.
3. Tell what they did differently, and what's different now, through their eyes.

The user is the main character; the product is what helped.

> [!FORM] Your post plan
> 1. Reader: who is this post for?
> 2. Action: what should they do? Read, reply, share, follow or buy.
> 3. Takeaway: what does someone walk away with? One sentence.

> [!DO] Write a three-line post plan
> Take your next post idea and fill in the post plan above. Cut anything in the draft that doesn't serve those three lines, then download the plan.
