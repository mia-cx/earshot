> [!TLDR]
> - The more you know, the harder it is to write for someone who doesn't. Pick one reader, one action and one takeaway before you write.
> - One idea per post. Three ideas are three posts.
> - Keep the idea advanced and the words simple enough for a 12-year-old. Experts prefer plain words too.
> - Show the change your product makes from the user's point of view.

## Pick the action before you write

**Decide the action before you write the first word.** Copywriting is getting the right person to take one action: read, reply, share, follow or buy. Everything in the post serves that action.

Naming your reader, action and takeaway first guards against the curse of knowledge. Once you know something, it's hard to imagine not knowing it. Chip and Dan Heath show this in *Made to Stick* with [Elizabeth Newton's Stanford study](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/getting-attention): people tapping out well-known songs expected listeners to name half, yet listeners named 3 out of 120.

The "right person" is the reader you pictured in *Say what you are, for whom, against what*. The action gets its own lesson in *Ask for one thing at the end*.

## Keep one idea per post

One idea per post is easier to remember and easier to pass on. If you have three ideas, you have three posts.

It also helps the feed. X's [published SimClusters code](https://github.com/twitter/the-algorithm/blob/main/src/scala/com/twitter/simclusters_v2/README.md) groups accounts into about 145,000 communities by who follows whom, then places each post by who likes it. One author reads this to mean a post about three topics fits none of them well. The code lets a post span several communities, so that's a guess, not a tested result. *How X picks what people see* covers the feed in detail.

Every post needs three things: a specific reader, a clear takeaway and a specific call to action.

## Choose clarity over cleverness

Good writing removes friction. Use words a 12-year-old can read, even when the idea is advanced. Keep the concept intact; simplify the words and formatting.

Experts want plain words too. Hoa Loranger at [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/) tested pages with experts in science, technology and medicine, and they also preferred short, plain, scannable text. NN/g suggests a 6th to 8th grade reading level (ages 11 to 14) for a general audience: "No one has ever complained that a text was too easy to understand."

Before you post, ask: "What does someone walk away with?" If you can't say it in a sentence, the post isn't ready.

## Write for the layperson and the expert

Picture a magician doing a trick nobody has ever done. A non-magician says "whoa, cool." The magician sitting next to them loses their mind, because they know it's never been done.

Aim for that. Write something a layperson finds interesting and an expert finds ten times more so. Copy for a general audience still lands with experts, as Loranger's study shows; copy written only for experts loses everyone else. Check each draft so a non-specialist would still stop for it.

## Tell the change from the user's side

A story only works when the product makes a real change in someone's life. As the [Christensen Institute's jobs-to-be-done research](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/) puts it, people don't just buy products; they "pull them into their lives to make progress".

1. Find the moment things changed for one user.
2. Describe their situation just before it.
3. Show what they did differently, and what's different now, through their eyes.

The user is the main character. The product is what helped.

> [!FORM] Your post plan
> 1. Reader: who is this post for?
> 2. Action: what should they do? Read, reply, share, follow or buy.
> 3. Takeaway: what does someone walk away with? One sentence.

> [!DO] Write a three-line post plan
> Take your next post idea and fill in the post plan above. Cut anything in the draft that doesn't serve those three lines, then download the plan.

> [!CHECK] Why do people who know a topic well often write posts their readers can't follow?
> The curse of knowledge: once you know something, it's hard to imagine not knowing it. Naming one reader, one action and one takeaway before you write is the check.

> [!CHECK] Your draft post covers three separate ideas. What do you do?
> Split it into three posts. One idea per post is easier to remember and pass on.

> [!CHECK] On X, how does a post find its interest communities?
> By who engages with it: X places a post among communities of shared interests by who likes it. That a mixed-topic post reaches fewer of the right people is one author's reading, not something X has shown.
