> [!TLDR]
> - A consistent, recognisable voice lets readers tell at a glance that a post is yours. Hooks and formats are shared; your voice is much harder to copy.
> - AI output always looks polished, and people can't reliably tell it from human writing, so polish tells you nothing about quality.
> - Publish only what you'd sign your name to.
> - Use AI as an editor that checks your drafts against rules, or as a drafter that learns from your edits.

## Your voice is the part nobody can copy

Anyone can copy your format or your hook types. It's much harder to copy **how you sound**. That makes voice the one lasting difference between you and everyone posting about the same thing.

Jenni Romaniuk of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute explains why. [In her words](https://contagious.cz/images/IO/jenni-romaniuk-on-distinctive-assets.pdf), distinctiveness is "looking like you and not like others", so people recognise your work at once and file it under your name. Shared cues are weaker, and changing a cue while you build it splits it into several weak ones. Don't make your voice a "fashion item".

- Write like you talk.
- Have opinions.
- Share what actually happened to you.
- Choose how you want to come across (smart, funny, blunt) and stay consistent with it.
- Keep your quirks.
- Don't copy someone else's voice.

To pick your tone, start with Kate Moran's [four scales at NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions/): funny to serious, formal to casual, respectful to irreverent, and enthusiastic to matter-of-fact. [In her tests](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users/), casual, conversational and moderately enthusiastic tones did best. A playful tone made an insurance company seem less trustworthy, and Moran calls humour "extremely risky". If you choose funny, test it on real readers.

## Polish is no longer a signal

AI output arrives tidy and confident, so polish no longer signals quality. Readers can't spot it either: across six experiments with 4,600 people, [Maurice Jakesch, Jeffrey Hancock and Mor Naaman](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07271) found people could not reliably tell AI-written text from human writing.

Judge every draft by your own standard. If you wouldn't sign your name to it, don't publish it. Addy Osmani says most of their own AI-made creative work stays unpublished for this reason.

## Use AI as an editor, not an author

Write the draft yourself and let AI check it against your rules. In a 2024 study of 300 writers, [Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290) found stories written from AI-generated ideas scored better one by one, yet came out more alike. Their writers still wrote every word; the study only tested AI ideas. Writing first and using AI as an editor is this course's suggestion for keeping the ideas yours, not a tested result.

Load a writing guide into a Claude Project and let it critique your drafts. The source post uses Ryan's [copywriting masterclass](https://x.com/ryangrowthh/status/2118723940638138486), which the earlier lessons in this module draw from, and offers no evidence beyond the guide author's own growth.

> [!TRICK] Turn a writing guide into a post editor
> 1. Paste the full guide into a Claude Project.
> 2. Drop in your next 5 drafts.
> 3. Have it rewrite each hook 10 different ways using the guide's rules.
> 4. Make it point out the line where a reader would stop.
> 5. Post the version you'd stop scrolling for yourself.

## Let a drafting bot learn from your edits

If you want AI to write first drafts, keep the final pass for yourself and make the bot learn from it. Each week it compares its drafts with what you published and turns your most common edits into new style-guide rules. The author used to spend close to half their time rewriting AI output into plain English and is aiming for 20%, with no result against that target reported yet.

> [!TRICK] Set up a Writer bot that learns from your edits
> Fill in the brackets and give this to your AI assistant:
>
> ```
> Role: you are my Writer. You draft. I do the last pass.
> Read first: [style guide], [banned-words list], my 10 best [posts / emails] in [folder].
> When you run: whenever I drop an idea or a call note into [Writer inbox doc].
> Done looks like: lines a person would say out loud, zero jargon, at least one number or example taken from my notes, plus 2 options for the first line.
> Every Friday, compare your drafts with what I published. Show me the 5 edits I made most, written as new lines for my style guide.
> Never: invent a result, a client name or a number I didn't give you.
> Ask me first before you: publish, schedule or send anything.
> ```

*Give each bot one job* covers how to set up bots like this one safely.

> [!FORM] Your voice card
> 1. How do you want to come across? Pick one: smart, funny or blunt.
> 2. Which opinion do you hold that others in your field don't?
> 3. Which second opinion do you hold that others in your field don't?
> 4. Which story actually happened to you?
> 5. Which quirk will you keep?

> [!DO] Write a five-line voice card
> Fill in your voice card above, one line each, then download it. Keep it open next to your drafts.

> [!CHECK] Why does a voice that stays the same help readers remember you?
> It makes you distinctive: readers can tell at a glance a post is yours and file it under your name. Changing it while you build it splits it into several weaker cues.

> [!CHECK] An AI draft looks polished and confident. How do you decide whether to publish it?
> Judge it against your own standard: publish it only if you'd put your name on it. AI output always looks polished, and people can't reliably tell it from human writing, so polish tells you nothing.

> [!CHECK] When you use AI for your writing, what role should it play if you write the drafts yourself?
> An editor that holds your drafts to a set of writing rules, for example by rewriting hooks and pointing out where a reader would stop. Drafting from AI ideas makes writing more alike.
