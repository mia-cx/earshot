> [!TLDR]
> - Use the first call to find out if they need it, can pay and can buy. Listen more than you talk.
> - Ask for money early. Give a buyer a way out of a paid deal rather than starting with a free trial.
> - Work out what the product is worth with the person who wants to buy it. Price from that, with your costs as a floor.
> - Ask who signs. If they need a pilot, agree what counts as success before you start.

## Find out if they can buy

On the first call, Pete Koomen's advice is to **qualify the buyer, not pitch the product**. Ask: "How do you solve this today?", "What can you spend to fix it?" and "Who decides and who signs?" If there's a fit, book a demo around their problem. If not, say so.

Tyler Bosmeny, who co-founded Clever, says the best salespeople he watched listened about 70% of the time. Aim for a quick yes or no. "We'd buy if you added this one feature" is usually a pass. Ask if they'd commit if you shipped it. If they won't, wait until other buyers ask for it too.

## Ask for money early

A free user can give feedback, but they haven't shown they'll pay. Gustaf Alströmer's advice for founder-led B2B sales: charge up front, then offer a money-back guarantee or a clear opt-out. The buyer can take a chance on you without having to make a second buying decision if it works.

## Price what it's worth to them

Work out the value with your champion, the person inside the company who wants your product. How many hours will it save, or how much cost will it cut or revenue will it add? Write down the assumptions together so they can defend the number to their boss. YC's Tom Blomfield typically quotes 25% to 50% of that value. If you both expect to save $20,000 a year, that's a quote of $5,000 to $10,000 a year. Your cost to serve them is the floor, not how you set the price.

> [!TRICK] Test the price on new pitches
> If you can't put a number on the value yet, Blomfield suggests starting near what buyers pay for similar software. Quote 50% more to each new prospect until price alone costs you more than a quarter of deals. That's a rough price range to test, not a rule to run on three calls. Count a deal here only when price was the reason they said no.

## Get it signed

Ask your champion what they can approve themselves and who else has to sign off. If the buyer needs a proof of concept, agree on a metric, a target and a decision date before you start. If they're ready to buy, Blomfield suggests an annual agreement with a 30- or 60-day opt-out. Put the deadline and how to cancel in writing so neither side has to guess.

> [!RISK] Ask about security and legal early
> If your buyers need a SOC 2 report, start the work early. Waiting for it can hold up a deal for months. Don't spend weeks fighting harmless wording. Get help with terms that can put the business at risk, like unlimited liability or handing over ownership of your product.

> [!FORM] Your next sales call
> 1. What's the buyer's problem, and what will you ask to check how urgent it is?
> 2. What will you ask about budget and who can sign?
> 3. What result would make this worth paying for, and what's your next ask?

> [!CHECK] What are you trying to learn on the first call?
> Whether the problem is real and urgent, whether there's budget, and who can decide and sign.

> [!CHECK] What do you price from, and what sets the floor?
> Price from the value you and your champion agree on. Your cost to serve the customer sets the floor.

> [!CHECK] What do you agree on before a proof of concept?
> The metric, the target it has to hit, and when the buyer will decide.

> [!DO] Prep one call
> Pick one buyer and fill in the three answers above. Keep them open on the call.
