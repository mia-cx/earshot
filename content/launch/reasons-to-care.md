> [!TLDR]
> - People share what surprises them, helps them or stirs a strong feeling. An announcement does none of that.
> - Feed posts get little time: about 1.7 seconds on average in Facebook's mobile feed. Lead with why a stranger should care; "We're excited to announce" never does.
> - Give strangers something to add: a demo they can examine, dispute or try.
> - Before you write copy, answer the narrative questions below to put your product inside a conversation people already have.

## Lead with why they should care

Every launch post hits one filter first: **why do I care?** Meta's 2016 [feed attention research](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/insights/capturing-attention-feed-video-creative) found people spend about 1.7 seconds on a mobile News Feed item on average. That's an average for one platform, not a deadline, but it shows how little time a post gets.

We also know what makes people pass a post on. Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman [tracked three months of New York Times articles](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.10.0353) and found people share surprising, useful and high-arousal content (awe, anger, anxiety) more, and share content that leaves them sad or flat less.

Announcement copy and feature details stir none of that, so people scroll right past.

What fails:

- "We're excited to announce"
- logo animations
- launches of a niche category nobody outside it has heard of
- 12 seconds of a founder talking before anything happens

What stops people: a funding number ("$20M raise" signals it matters), a fight people already have opinions on (datacenters, OpenAI), or a result so big it sounds fake. Each one triggers surprise or strong feeling.

Launch agency founder Matt Epstein says launch comments rarely ask about features, in his experience. People congratulate the raise or argue over what it means. Decide what they will argue about before you write.

> [!TRICK] Watch people scroll
> Get on a subway or bus and watch people on their phones. Note the moment each one stops on a post, and what caught their eye. That is the filter your launch has to pass.

> [!TRICK] Three first lines that stop strangers
> Leo (@leodev), who launches developer tools on X and writes the free weekly letter [lessons.sh](https://lessons.sh), writes line 1 for someone who has never heard of the product. Pick the opener that fits:
>
> | Cut a first line like | Start with instead |
> | --- | --- |
> | "Introducing [name], a new open-source tool I built..." | The annoying problem it removes: "every online file converter makes you upload your files to a random server" |
> | "Excited to announce v0.2 of our SDK with..." | The test you ran, and how many providers you measured |
> | "Built a fast, lightweight library in Rust for..." | What someone can now do, in one sentence |
>
> Name the product on line 2. Say how to use it on line 3, in a few words: "right-click any file, pick a format, done".

## Give strangers something to add

Quotes spread your launch, and strangers only quote when they have something to add. Announcements give them nothing to work with. Concrete material they can examine, dispute or try gives them a reason to chime in.

Skip "Excited to announce our new platform" and show a specific demo. Turn a messy inventory spreadsheet into a working app, showing the input, the output and what you had to fix by hand. Those hand fixes make it believable and give people a detail to discuss.

Before you publish, ask: **what could a stranger add to this?** If you can't answer, add something more specific. *Line up your launch-day voices* covers how to seed the angles they might take.

Numbers work the same way. "Blazingly fast" and "way cheaper" give nobody anything to check, while a comparison people can verify is useful, and Berger and Milkman found useful content gets shared more.

> [!TRICK] Make a comparison people can check
> 1. Anchor on one workload everyone in your field knows. The Sume API launch didn't claim "up to 80% cheaper across models"; it compared prices on one popular video model.
> 2. Stack the competitors' numbers in a vertical list (Higgsfield 40%, fal 100%, Runway 414%, Sume 0% markup). A vertical list reads in a second on a phone.
> 3. Show the receipts: the Sume video walks through each competitor's pricing calculator on screen.
> 4. Put the benchmark's code in the first reply, so skeptics can run it themselves.
>
> Free projects can do the same with latency, bundle size or hosting cost. Leo reports 137.8K views for his benchmark of 5 object storage providers from 6 regions.

## Plant the narrative before the copy

Start from a conversation people already have, then put your product inside it. Tie your claim to something people already feel so it reads bigger than one product.

Answer these questions before you write launch copy. The enemy builds on what you're against in *Say what you are, for whom, against what*, so your answer from there comes first. The customers' exact words are what *Research a market in an afternoon* collects.

> [!FORM] Your launch narrative
> 1. What does the market already love?
> 2. What does the market already hate?
> 3. What are we against? Name a status quo, a bad habit or an incumbent way of doing things.
> 4. Who is the enemy?
> 5. What is the highest-status outcome?
> 6. What exact words do customers use?
> 7. What is the most polarizing thing you can say that's true?
> 8. What could a stranger add to this?

In Epstein's example, a hospital intake and notes software launch started as "first step toward medical superintelligence, $24M raised, 10M+ patients". True, yet any health-AI company could say it. The rewrite gave four groups a reason to reply: patients told "it's probably nothing", families who lost someone to a late diagnosis, clinicians, and people who argue about AI. His agency reports 1.5M views on day one and a trending launch (their own claim).

> [!RISK] Anger spreads, and it sticks to you
> Berger and Milkman found anger shares as well as awe. A polarizing line buys reach, but people remember the fight along with your product. Only say what's true and what you can defend with evidence.

## Treat each launch as a small go-to-market

A launch is not one post. Each one needs its own ideal customer, its own channels, a plan to scale what works, and a launch video. Kunal Gandhi, a former marketer at Eigen Labs, lists skipping this as a common launch mistake and points to thirdweb's launches as an example done well.

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares teach the same habit in [*Traction*](https://leananalyticsbook.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Traction-1-3.pdf). Test a few channels cheaply, then focus on the one that works. A launch is one of those tests.

> [!RISK] One launch rarely makes a product
> Paul Graham [calls the Big Launch](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) a tactic that "usually doesn't work". All you need from a launch is an initial core of users, so plan the weeks after it, not just the day.

**Launch something small every week.** The same kind of post can swing wildly on timing and who is online in its first 20 minutes. Leo's second storage benchmark, with the same hook and chart, got 18.5K views against the first one's 137.8K, by his count. A quiet day sinks a launch you spent three months on. With one small demoable piece a week, each launch adds people who recognise your name on the next.

A launch is one moment. Your brand is what people see the rest of the year (see *Build a brand, not a viral hit*). The launch gets the spike, and your brand keeps the people who show up.
