> [!TLDR]
> - Awe and surprise are among the strongest reasons people share. The demo is where you create them.
> - Show the most extreme thing your product can do, in a way nobody can dismiss as fake.
> - In a feed, show the product, make it work without sound, and keep it short.

## The demo earns belief

A good narrative makes someone stop scrolling (see *Give strangers a reason to care*). Then the demo has a different job: make the viewer think "this is real". If it looks staged or vague, the stop is wasted.

Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman's [research on sharing](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.10.0353) found that content evoking awe or surprise gets passed on more. A demo can create both, but only if people believe it.

## Push the product to its extreme

**Show the most extreme thing your product can do**, then make the proof impossible to wave away.

Matt Epstein uses Tesla's Roadster launch as an example. The car drives up a curved ramp, hangs upside down, flips through the air, lands on its wheels, then jumps over a whole row of Teslas. Jumping one car is interesting; jumping the fleet is something you send to ten friends. And it was done live, in front of a crowd, on a livestream. [Snopes checked it](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/tesla-flying-roadster-car-real/): Tesla streamed the event on 15 October 2026, attendees posted their own clips, and Elon Musk said on stage, "We need an audience to prove that it's not AI."

You don't need a stunt budget. You need two answers, then film only the moments that answer both.

> [!FORM] Your demo shot list
> 1. What is the most extreme thing your product can do?
> 2. How do you make people believe it happened?
> 3. Which three moments would you film to prove both? One line each.

For software, show the real input, the real output and anything you fixed by hand (the spreadsheet-to-app example in *Give strangers a reason to care*). Better still, let people try it themselves. Hacker News's [Show HN rules](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html) ask for exactly that: something people can play with, ideally without a sign-up.

## Learn video as a medium

Kunal Gandhi argues you should master video as a way to communicate, whatever platform it lands on. The skill is knowing what captures your audience's imagination on screen. In Gandhi's experience, product previews, demo videos and quirky humour outperform everything else, and launches gain the most from video. The post points to thirdweb's launch videos as a model.

Meta's research backs the product part. In a [study of over 600 video ads](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/insights/capturing-attention-feed-video-creative), people remembered ads best when they showed the product and kept the message short. Meta describes feed viewing as fast and sound-free, so a feed video has to make sense on mute.

> [!TRICK] Cut the demo for the feed
> 1. Open on the extreme moment, not on a logo or a founder intro.
> 2. Add captions or on-screen text so it works on mute.
> 3. Cut anything that doesn't prove the feat.

Start with a screen recording of the extreme moment. When you want it to look premium, see *Make product videos feel premium*.

> [!DO] Write your demo's shot list
> Fill in your demo shot list above: the two answers, then the three moments you would film to prove them. Then download it.

> [!CHECK] What two questions should a launch demo answer?
> What is the most extreme thing your product can do, and how do you make people believe it happened? Film only the moments that answer both.

> [!CHECK] Viewers say your software launch demo looks staged. What do you show instead?
> The real input, the real output, and anything you fixed by hand. A launch demo's job is to make people think "this is real", and the hand fixes make it believable.

> [!CHECK] Why did Tesla jump the Roadster over a whole row of cars, live in front of a crowd?
> Jumping the whole fleet is extreme enough to send to friends, and doing it live made it impossible to dismiss as CGI or AI.
