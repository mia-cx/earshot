> [!TLDR]
> - When people are unsure, they look to others like them. A launch that many people vouch for at once gives them that proof.
> - Ask people with an audience to quote your launch instead of retweeting it, and line them up to post at the same moment.
> - Seed different angles (founder story, implications, a sceptical try-out) so each quote reaches a different crowd.
> - Pick mid-sized creators who fit over big ones, brief them to write their own take, and never organise upvotes on Product Hunt, or upvotes and comments on Hacker News.

> [!DIAGRAM] launch-timeline

## Why many voices beat one

Robert Cialdini calls this [social proof](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/). When people are uncertain, they look to what others do, especially people like them. In his hotel study, a card saying most guests in that same room reused their towels beat every other message.

A launch is a moment of uncertainty for everyone who sees it. When people they already follow are talking about it, they have a reason to look. Your job on launch day is to get the right people talking, each in their own words.

## Ask for quotes, not retweets

A follower sees your posts. A connection has an audience and answers your text. **When a connection quotes your launch, it becomes a new post from them** in their followers' feeds. In Matt Epstein's experience, a retweet does far less.

Keep the launch in one post so every quote points to the same place. X shows one post per conversation, so a launch split across five posts loses most of them (see *How X picks what people see*).

Epstein frames the ask with a story from Opendoor co-founder JD Ross. Asking someone powerful for an intro spends a favour and gets you one meeting. Asking them to quote your launch spends the same favour and reaches their whole audience.

> [!TRICK] Build and tier your quote list
> 1. Export the followers of everyone on your team who has an account.
> 2. Add the people no export shows: investors, advisors, customers, beta testers, portfolio founders.
> 3. Rank everyone by how far they reach into your customer.
> 4. The top ~25 get a personal message from the founder.
>
> - Bad: "Hey, we're launching Tuesday, would love a retweet!"
> - Good: "Hey, here's what we've been working on. Would you quote this? Here's an example of a great one from an investor on a similar launch."
>
> Epstein's agency claims 200M views across 30+ X launches and 2,000+ booked demos, with one launch (WithCoverage) booking 600. These are the agency's own unverified numbers, and it sells launch services.

## Post together, on a story people already follow

Kunal Gandhi calls this a **thunderclap**. Founders, friends and partners all post about your launch at the same set time, lined up in advance.

Attach your launch to a conversation already moving rather than fighting it. Anchor it to the platform or ecosystem you build on, and use your integrations as the onboarding story. In Gandhi's experience, getting bigger players in your ecosystem to notice you beats paid campaigns.

> [!RISK] Never ask for upvotes on Product Hunt or Hacker News
> A thunderclap of posts on X is fine. Organised voting is not. Product Hunt's [launch guide](http://help.producthunt.com/en/articles/17734384-5-how-to-promote-your-launch-and-what-to-avoid) says "Never ask for upvotes". Asking for or rewarding upvotes can get your launch removed, and votes from new accounts barely count. Hacker News's [Show HN rules](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html) say "Please don't ask friends to upvote or comment." On Product Hunt, ask people to try it and share feedback instead. On Hacker News, only ask them to try it.

## Seed different angles

Each angle pulls in a different crowd, so plan the takes others could run with before launch day.

> [!TRICK] Five quote angles to seed
> - **Founder story:** why they built it, a personal problem or an unusual path.
> - **Implications:** which part of someone's work changes, who benefits, why a feature matters more than it looks.
> - **Video reaction:** someone tries it on camera, pauses on the interesting parts, says where they're sceptical.
> - **Controversial take:** one specific implication someone will defend, with enough evidence to be challenged.
> - **Competing interests:** who saves money and whose business is threatened; who gains independence and who has to maintain it.

> [!RISK] Controversy sticks to the product
> A controversial angle shapes what people believe about your product long after launch. Only invite disagreement your team can answer with evidence.

## Pick creators for fit, not reach

Mediocre influencers deliver numbers that turn into nothing, Kunal Gandhi warns. A few well-matched ones shape the right narrative and bring the right audience.

Research backs the part about reach. Simone Wies, Alexander Bleier and Alexander Edeling [studied over 800 Instagram campaigns](https://www.ama.org/2022/10/11/finding-goldilocks-influencers-why-high-follower-count-may-not-be-the-best-driver-of-engagement-on-social-media/) and found engagement rises with follower count only up to a point, then falls. Mid-sized creators were the sweet spot because followers of huge accounts feel a weaker tie to them. Big creators did better when they made original content in their own style.

Fit means the creator lives with the problem and their audience would plausibly try your product. Read their posts and replies first to see who pays attention and what questions come up. Give them real access and let them write their own take. If every creator posts the same paragraph, you've lost the point.

Brief each creator in five lines: what the product does, what they can test themselves, the part their audience would care about, the evidence behind the claim, and the closing question, "What's your take after trying it?"

> [!FORM] Your creator brief
> 1. What do we do? What problem do we solve? Give one short, precise answer.
> 2. What can the creator test themselves?
> 3. Which part do you think their audience would care about?
> 4. What's the evidence behind the claim?

Offer a call if they need help understanding it.

> [!RISK] Undisclosed paid posts
> The FTC's [guide for influencers](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) says any financial, employment, personal or family relationship needs disclosure, including free access to your product. Put the disclosure in the post itself, not only in a bio. US law applies to any post likely to reach US buyers, and other countries have their own rules.

> [!DO] Draft your top-25 quote list
> In a spreadsheet, list 25 people who could quote your next launch, with one column for their reach into your customer and one for the angle you'd ask them to take.

> [!CHECK] In a product launch, what is a thunderclap?
> Founders, friends and partners all post about the launch at the same set time. You line up the list in advance.

> [!CHECK] You were about to message an investor "We're launching Tuesday, would love a retweet". What do you ask for instead?
> Ask them to quote the launch post, and show them an example of a great quote from a similar launch. A quote becomes a new post shown to their followers, while a retweet does far less.

> [!CHECK] When you pick creators to post about your launch, why choose fit over reach?
> Engagement peaks with mid-sized creators, because followers of huge accounts feel a weaker tie. A few who live with the problem bring the right audience and shape the right story.
