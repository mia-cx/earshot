> [!TLDR]
> - Google judges a link by its purpose: links made mainly to move rankings are spam, and Google's SpamBrain detects and neutralises them.
> - Paying for roundup spots, swapping links, buying "best X" domains and using press-release sites can all work, and can all cost you.
> - Paid links that pass ranking credit, excessive link exchanges, and keyword links in press releases are all named as link spam.
> - Decide your line before you're tempted, and write it down.

## Know where Google draws the line

**[Google's spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#link-spam) define link spam by purpose: links created "primarily for the purpose of manipulating search rankings".** Paid links are allowed only when marked `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`, which stops them passing ranking credit. "Excessive link exchanges" are named as a link scheme outright.

Enforcement is mostly quiet. Since [Google's December 2022 link spam update](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2022/12/december-22-link-spam-update), SpamBrain detects sites that buy and sell links and neutralises those links, so "any credit passed" is lost. Usually, a bought link is money for nothing. At worst, a pattern of them can earn a [manual action](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044175) for unnatural links, which can push down some or all of your site.

None of this means these tactics never work. The upside is real readers; the ranking credit is the part most likely to vanish. Weigh each one on its own.

> [!RISK] What gets you burned
> TheoAGI's list of what to refuse outright, from 66 SEO articles:
> - Link exchange and credit networks. Even the articles selling them admit Google calls it link spam.
> - Paid Reddit and Quora "tasks", fake accounts, and reviews you wrote yourself.
> - 1,500 AI posts spread across a network of sites.
> - City × service template pages.

## Pay for a spot in a roundup

One bootstrapped SaaS builder says cited sources for buying queries are mostly listicles, and the boring way in is to pay. Research puts roundups as the largest single type of AI citation, not the majority, and others in this course push the earned route instead (see *Give people a reason to link*).

> [!TRICK] The direct paid pitch
> 1. Search Google or ChatGPT for a question your buyers ask, where you want to be recommended.
> 2. Open the sources both show. Note the listicles naming competitors but not you.
> 3. Have an agent find the emails of the people running them.
> 4. Email each one. Skip the flattery ("I just saw your blogpost and it really stood out"). Say how you found the list, that you'd like your product in it, and that you're happy to pay.
> 5. Expect some rejections and some yeses. Repeat.
>
> The same author warns against backlink marketplaces: "don't use them."

> [!RISK] Unmarked paid links
> A paid link that passes ranking credit is link spam under Google's policies unless it carries `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`. Cold emails must follow CAN-SPAM and GDPR. If you pay, ask for the sponsored label and value the spot for its readers and AI citations, not ranking credit.

## Swap links inside a relevant network

A link exchange is two sites agreeing to link to each other in their articles. One SEO ranks it top-tier, and also sells a link-exchange product, so weigh that ranking accordingly.

> [!TRICK] A relevant editorial exchange
> Join a business network in your field. Match each link to the topic around it, link to the specific answer the reader needs, and log the source and destination URL of every link delivered.

> [!RISK] Excessive exchanges
> Google lists "excessive link exchanges" as a link scheme. Keep any swap rare, relevant and useful to readers on both sides.

## Buy exact-match "best X" domains

One investor claims domains matching "best X" searches word for word ranked #1 on Google and in AI answers within 10 days in the data-labelling niche, drawing a seven-figure acquisition offer. No URLs, traffic or rankings were shown, and a domain broker amplified the post.

> [!TRICK] The claimed playbook
> 1. Find a niche with heavy spending, urgent need, high ticket sizes, and buyers and sellers who struggle to find each other. The author's example: buying and selling AI training data.
> 2. Register domains that match queries one to one: "best data labs", "best ai data labs", "best data labelling", "best data labelling for ai", "best data labelling platform", "best data labelling software", "best data labelling tools", "best data brokers", "best enterprise fine tuning services".
> 3. Publish relevant content on them, consistently tie the entity to those keywords, and wait.
>
> The logic: when one lead is worth a lot, owning the "best X" answer is worth renting or selling.

> [!RISK] Google discounts exact-match domains
> [Google's ranking systems guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ranking-systems-guide) lists an exact-match domain system, built so domains "designed to exactly match particular queries" don't get too much credit. Its [SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) says keywords in a domain have "hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs". If those domains ranked, the name is unlikely to be why. The method's details ("lexical proximity", "entity mapping") are vague. Treat this as a bet with domain fees as the stake, not a plan.

## Use press-release sites only for real news

Press-release sites host your announcement; they aren't news coverage. Use them only for a genuine launch, study or milestone.

> [!TRICK] Press-release sites
> PRLog, openPR, PR.com, IssueWire, 1888PressRelease, Express Press Release, Pressbox, PressReleaseBox, PressReleasePoint, Press Release Power, PRFree, Circle Acts, Press Release Submit, PR News Releaser, SubmitPR. Some limit free publication or clickable links.

> [!RISK] Little SEO value
> A hosted release carries little SEO value and isn't independent coverage. Google's spam policies name "links with optimized anchor text in articles, guest posts, or press releases distributed on other sites" as link spam. Link with your brand name or plain URL, don't count it as press, and don't pay for distribution expecting rankings.

> [!CHECK] When does Google allow a paid link?
> Only when it's marked `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`, so it passes no ranking credit. An unmarked paid link counts as link spam.

> [!CHECK] A roundup owner will add your product for a fee. If you pay, what do you ask for, and what are you buying?
> Ask for the sponsored label. You're buying the spot's readers and AI citations, not ranking credit.

> [!CHECK] Why treat buying exact-match "best X" domains as a bet rather than a plan?
> The evidence is one unverified claim, amplified by a domain broker. Google also runs a system that stops exact-match domains getting too much credit, and says keywords in a domain have hardly any effect.
