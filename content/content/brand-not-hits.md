> [!TLDR]
> - A spike of attention fades fast; a brand builds over months and years. One viral post followed by silence means the post worked and the brand doesn't exist.
> - Post about what you build and learn, 3 to 5 times a week, on the one platform your people use.
> - Steady beats frequent. Expect six months or more before it pays off.
> - Content fails when it's irregular, unplanned, hard to follow or ignores what people are talking about.

## One hit is not a brand

Short-term spikes fade fast; a brand builds over years. In [*The Long and the Short of It*](https://ipa.co.uk/media/5811/long_and_short_of_it_presentation_final.pdf), Les Binet and Peter Field analysed 996 campaigns in the IPA effectiveness databank. Short-term pushes gave a big jump in sales that decayed quickly. Brand building gave a smaller jump that decayed slowly, built up over years, and drove growth and profit. They studied ad campaigns, not posts, and say a brand needs both kinds.

A post with a million views followed by posts at a thousand works like the short-term kind: the post worked, and nobody cares who wrote it. Dan Koe's aim is **a body of work people want to explore**: they read one post, then go looking for more.

The test: if you stopped posting, would anyone ask where you went? Koe plays a brand's ideas out over 6 to 12 months of posts, so no single post has to carry everything.

## Three jobs sit behind every brand

Koe splits content work into three roles. Most people skip the first one and post random ideas.

- Brand strategist: decides how you want to be seen, and puts trust and authority ahead of chasing trends.
- Content strategist: watches what performs (formats, YouTube title and thumbnail structures, retention) and proposes what to post next.
- Writer or social manager: drafts, schedules and posts in line with both.

Working alone, you hold all three. *Write down what makes you you* covers the brand job, and *Plan six months of ideas in one sitting* covers the content job.

## Post what you build, on one platform

When anyone can build something in a weekend, attention goes to people others already know and trust. Writing in public about your real work is the cheapest way to become one of them.

Joe Pulizzi, author of *Content Inc.*, calls starting on one platform "building the base". [In his words](https://www.magnificent.com/magnificent-stuff/content-inc-model), you "focus on one content type, and one platform, and consistently deliver on time." Other platforms come later, once the base works.

1. Pick the one platform where the people you want to reach spend time. For most builders that's X or LinkedIn.
2. Post 3 to 5 times a week about what you built and learned: an agent you made, a task you sped up, your exact setup.
3. Let AI research and draft. The experience, opinions and results must be yours.
4. Keep going for at least six months before you judge it. After that, people come to you, which makes every other skill worth more.

The 3 to 5 times a week is @notkvtwts's opinion, not a measured result.

> [!RISK] It takes longer than a few months
> @notkvtwts says to expect a few months. Pulizzi's own experience was slower: "radio silence" for the first six months, the first signs between months six and nine, and no direct revenue until after twelve. Plan for that, so a quiet third month doesn't make you quit.

## Start from your own account

Early on, a founder's own account can be the strongest growth channel. That's Kunal Gandhi's view, not a study: "Your founder's X presence is your strongest growth channel."

While building a marketing team at an AI startup, Kunal made social media training part of new-hire onboarding, starting with the CEO. The team "achieved a lot more through social media than we thought". If you have a team, every account is a channel.

## Steady beats often

Kunal says post daily; @notkvtwts says 3 to 5 times a week. Pick the number you can keep up for months. **Show up every week**, and raise the number later.

A [2025 Buffer study](https://buffer.com/resources/consistent-posting-study/) tracked over 100,000 accounts across 26 weeks. People who posted in at least 20 of those weeks got more than 5 times the engagement per post of those who posted in 4 weeks or fewer, and after about 21 weeks the gain levelled off, then dipped. It counted weeks with any post, not posts per week, so it can't settle daily versus three times a week. It's also a correlation from a company that sells scheduling tools. It points the same way as Pulizzi: on time, every week, beats a burst.

## Four ways content fails

From Kunal's list of content mistakes:

- Inconsistent: good posts with long gaps between them.
- Missing the conversation: ignoring the popular topics your content could join.
- No distribution plan: publishing and hoping people find it.
- Hard to understand: posts readers struggle to follow. *Write for one reader and one action* fixes this one.

> [!FORM] Your first three posts
> 1. Where do they read? Are they even on X?
> 2. Which one platform do the people you want to reach spend time on?
> 3. Post 1: a draft about one thing you built, fixed or learned this week.
> 4. Post 2: a draft about another thing you built, fixed or learned this week.
> 5. Post 3: a draft about a third thing you built, fixed or learned this week.

> [!DO] Write your first three posts
> Fill in your first three posts above: name your one platform, then write three drafts. Download them when you're done.
